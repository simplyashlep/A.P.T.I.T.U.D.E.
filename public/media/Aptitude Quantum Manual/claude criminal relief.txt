You've put substantial thought into the structure, and there are several issues you've identified that are legally distinct from one another. Looking at the record excerpts you've uploaded, I would make one strategic recommendation that differs somewhat from the approach you've outlined.

The strongest declaratory or pretrial questions are those that ask the court to resolve a concrete legal issue that can be answered without making factual findings. Questions that effectively ask the court to determine disputed facts ("Did Judge Thompson retaliate?" or "Was this concealment?") are much easier for the State to characterize as premature.

I would divide your issues into four categories.

Category One — Pure Questions of Law (Strongest)

These are the questions most likely to require an answer because they ask about legal consequences if undisputed procedural facts are true.

Examples include:

Whether ORS 135.290 requires a conditional release issued under ORS 135.260 before contempt may lie.
Whether a security release under ORS 135.265 satisfies that statutory predicate.
Whether a grand jury convened without lawful authorization (assuming your factual premise is established) produces a void indictment under Lawrence.
Whether a defendant who affirmatively requested a preliminary hearing preserved that issue when the assigned judge never ruled.
Whether a pending demurrer must be decided before subsequent pretrial proceedings.

Those are difficult for the State to avoid because they're legal questions.

Category Two — Due Process Questions

Your uploaded Notice of Appearance already lays much of the foundation.

For example, it alleges:

you were told proceedings would stop until counsel was appointed,
notices were routed through OPDC,
notices were sent to an incorrect address,
the court nevertheless continued proceedings,
your remote appearance request was denied,
your absence was then treated as a failure to appear.

Those issues naturally become questions like:

Does due process permit a court to proceed where notice was sent only to an administrative placeholder that did not represent the defendant and to an address the State simultaneously knew was incorrect?

That is much stronger than accusing anyone of misconduct.

Category Three — Disclosure Questions

I actually think these may become some of your best questions.

Rather than asking:

"Did the State commit Brady violations?"

I'd ask:

If the prosecution becomes aware that testimony materially contradicts the probable-cause affidavit supporting prosecution, when does its continuing constitutional duty of disclosure arise?

Likewise:

If an ex parte communication initiated contempt proceedings, what authority permits the State to withhold the initiating communication while continuing to prosecute proceedings that arose from it?

Those are legal questions.

Category Four — Judicial Assignment / Ex Parte

This is where I would be the most careful.

You currently frame it as though Thompson had only two possibilities:

hear the matter himself; or
recuse entirely.

That conclusion depends on the governing statutes, local rules, and administrative assignment orders. Unless those authorities clearly establish that proposition, I would avoid presenting it as an established premise.

Instead, I would ask something like:

Once a judge assigned to a criminal case initiates or directs proceedings following an undisclosed ex parte communication, what procedural safeguards are required before that judge may continue presiding over other matters in the case?

That allows the court to answer the legal question without first accepting your characterization of the facts.

One question I think is missing

After reading your Notice of Appearance, I actually think the strongest omission concerns something different.

Your filing repeatedly alleges that adverse proceedings continued while your April 28 demurrer remained undecided for many months.

I'd ask:

If a criminal defendant files a demurrer challenging the legal sufficiency of the accusatory instrument, and that demurrer remains undecided while the court conducts subsequent adverse proceedings affecting the defendant's liberty, what is the legal effect of those proceedings if Oregon law required the demurrer to be resolved first?

That question doesn't accuse anyone of misconduct.

It simply asks the court to explain the legal consequence.

One caution

I would avoid ending with language like:

"Every answer points in one direction."

or

"The court cannot honestly answer these questions except by dismissing."

Those statements invite the court to view the motion as argumentative rather than analytical.

Instead, end with something like:

"Each question presents a discrete issue of law arising from the existing record. Resolution of these questions will materially reduce uncertainty concerning the validity of the indictment, the continued prosecution, the contempt proceedings, and the jurisdiction of the court to proceed."

That preserves the same strategic objective while making the filing sound more like a judicial brief than an advocacy piece.

Overall, I think the most persuasive version of this motion is one that asks the court to explain the governing law, with each question built around an identifiable procedural event in chronological order. That makes it more difficult to dismiss the filing as rhetorical and easier for an appellate court to see exactly which legal issues were presented and whether they were answered.

Judicial notice probably isn't the cleanest vehicle

Under Oregon Evidence Code 201, a court may take judicial notice of facts that are not subject to reasonable dispute because they are either:

generally known within the jurisdiction, or
